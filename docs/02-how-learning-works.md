# 2. How Learning Works (the short version)

You don't need a psychology degree. You need five findings that hold up across decades of research. Every tool in this kit exists to apply one of them.

## The core idea: memory is built by retrieval, not by exposure

Re-reading and re-watching feel productive. The material looks familiar, so your brain says "I know this." That feeling is **fluency**, and it lies. Familiar is not the same as retrievable. The exam asks you to *retrieve*.

## The five principles

### 1. Retrieval practice (the testing effect)
Pulling an answer out of memory strengthens that memory far more than reading it again. In a well-known study, students who practiced recalling a passage remembered much more a week later than students who re-read it, even though the re-readers *felt* more confident.
**In this kit:** flashcards, and the audio pauses where you answer out loud before you hear the answer.
*Source: Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, Psychological Science.*

### 2. Spacing
Reviews spread over days beat the same number of reviews crammed into one sitting. Each gap lets you forget a little, and each successful recall after a gap builds a stronger trace.
**In this kit:** Anki schedules reviews for you. Turn on FSRS (Deck Options → Advanced) for the modern scheduler.
*Source: Cepeda et al., 2006, Psychological Bulletin (meta-analysis).*

### 3. Interleaving
Mixing topics (a ports card, then a wireless card, then a troubleshooting card) beats studying one topic in a block. Mixing forces you to identify *which* kind of problem you face, and the exam does the same.
**In this kit:** study the parent deck, not one subdeck at a time, once you've seen every topic once.
*Source: Rohrer & Taylor, 2007, Instructional Science.*

### 4. Multiple encodings — "hit it from several angles"
Learn a fact through more than one channel: hear it, read it, draw it, say it, apply it. Each route is another path back to the memory. A picture plus words beats words alone (dual coding). Explaining it in your own words beats both (elaboration).
**In this kit:** the same fact shows up as an audio drill, a flashcard, a scenario card, and a practice-test question.
*Source: Paivio's dual coding theory; Dunlosky et al., 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest.*

### 5. Feedback on errors
A miss you review, understand, and re-test becomes one of your strongest memories. A miss you ignore repeats.
**In this kit:** the [adaptive loop](06-adaptive-loop.md) turns every practice-test miss into targeted cards and audio.

## A myth to drop: "I'm a visual learner"

The idea that each person learns best in one style — visual, auditory, kinesthetic — and should be taught only that way is popular. It has not held up. Researchers looked for the evidence and didn't find it (Pashler et al., 2008).

What *does* hold up is this: **everyone** learns better when material arrives through several channels and gets retrieved in several ways. Pick formats by what the content needs, not by a label. Port numbers suit audio drills. Subnetting needs pencil and paper. Topologies need a diagram.

So use audio **and** flashcards **and** practice tests. Not because you're an "audio learner," but because each angle adds a retrieval path.

## Why audio works for commuters

Audio is not magic. It works here for three practical reasons:

1. **It uses dead time.** A 30-minute commute each way is five hours a week.
2. **The pauses force retrieval.** You say the answer before you hear it. That is retrieval practice, not passive listening.
3. **Repetition is free.** Replaying a 10-minute drill costs nothing.

Audio is weak for anything visual or procedural. Subnetting, diagrams, and command output belong at a desk.

## What this looks like in a day

| Time | Activity | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Commute in (30 min) | Audio drill, answer out loud | Retrieval, spacing |
| Lunch (15 min) | Anki reviews on phone | Spacing, retrieval |
| Evening (60–90 min) | New content (video or reading) → make or import cards | Encoding |
| Evening (30 min) | Practice questions, mixed domains | Interleaving, feedback |
| Commute home (30 min) | Audio drill on today's misses | Feedback, retrieval |

## Further reading

- *Make It Stick* — Brown, Roediger, McDaniel. The best single book on this.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques." Rates ten study techniques. Practice testing and distributed practice score highest. Highlighting and re-reading score lowest.
